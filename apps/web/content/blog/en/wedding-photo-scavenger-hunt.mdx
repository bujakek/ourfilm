A wedding photo scavenger hunt gives guests a short list of people, details and
moments to notice with their cameras. The version that works is optional,
starts after any unplugged ceremony and asks people to capture moments that are
already happening. Choose 10–15 prompts from the 30 below rather than handing
every guest an exhausting checklist.

## The simple plan

Give each table one small prompt card and put the same tested QR code on every
copy. Announce the hunt once near the start of the reception, set a clear finish
time and tell guests whether the QR tool takes new photos, existing photos or
both. A scavenger hunt complements the professional photographer; it should
never send guests into an aisle or make them recreate an important moment.

Use these five rules:

1. Participation is optional.
2. Photograph natural moments without interrupting them.
3. Ask before taking a close portrait of someone you do not know.
4. Respect phone-free parts of the day and the photographer's working space.
5. Send every finished photo to the same private destination.

## Choose a format that fits the reception

The easiest format is one shared list of 10–15 prompts on every table. It needs
the least preparation, but several guests may photograph the same cake, shoes
and dance move. This works best for a small wedding where duplicate viewpoints
are still useful.

For more variety, divide the prompts into different table missions. Give each
table five or six unique prompts plus three easy prompts everyone receives.
Guests have a manageable card, while the finished collection covers more of the
room.

Photo bingo adds a visible goal without requiring anyone to complete the whole
list. Put nine prompts in a three-by-three grid and count a row as finished.
Skip a leaderboard unless your guests genuinely enjoy competition; a favourite
photo chosen later is easier to run.

## 30 wedding photo scavenger-hunt prompts

Treat this as a prompt bank. Remove anything that clashes with your schedule,
venue rules or guests, then add two personal references that only your group
will recognise.

### Arrivals and room details

1. Friends seeing each other after a long time apart
2. Someone finding their name on the seating plan
3. A guest noticing a tiny detail in the room
4. The view from your table
5. Something old, new, borrowed or blue
6. The most colourful outfit detail
7. A reflection that includes part of the celebration
8. Someone helping with a tie, necklace or button
9. The room before every seat is filled
10. A photograph with no faces in it

### Dinner, speeches and conversations

11. Someone laughing at another table
12. A reaction to a speech—not only the person speaking
13. Two guests who met for the first time today
14. Different generations sharing a story
15. A spontaneous toast between friends
16. A place setting halfway through dinner
17. Someone quietly helping the day run smoothly
18. A candid portrait taken with permission
19. The couple seen from your seat
20. A detail the couple may not have noticed

### Dance floor and late night

21. The first group to fill the dance floor
22. A dramatic but unplanned dance move
23. Shoes resting at the edge of the room
24. Someone singing every word
25. A friendship in one frame
26. The late-night snack arriving
27. A joyful photograph with motion blur
28. A quiet break away from the music
29. The last guests still dancing
30. A final goodbye at the end of the night

Prompts about reactions and relationships usually create a more personal album
than prompts that only name objects. They also leave room for guests to notice
their own part of the wedding instead of copying the formal shot list. For more
inspiration without the game format, see these [wedding guest photo
ideas](/en/blog/wedding-guest-photo-ideas).

## Time the hunt around the wedding

If the ceremony is unplugged, do not print ceremony prompts and hope guests
understand the exception. Start the hunt at cocktail hour or when guests enter
the reception instead. Put the cards where people naturally pause, such as at
their place settings or beside the guestbook.

Dinner is good for conversations, reactions and table details, but not for
tasks that require people to move around while food is served. Dance-floor
prompts work after the formal dances and speeches. Close the hunt before the
final departure if you plan to choose a favourite that night; otherwise let the
camera's capture window define the finish.

## Copy this wording onto the card

**Wedding photo scavenger hunt**

Spot any of these moments? Take a photo without interrupting it. Join in as much
or as little as you like, respect anyone who does not want to be photographed,
and stay out of the photographer's way. Scan the QR code to open our wedding
camera and follow the instructions on your phone.

That final sentence should match the tool you actually chose. With OurFilm, the

Your DJ or MC can use this 20-second announcement:

> There is a photo scavenger hunt on your table. If you notice one of the
> moments on your card, take a picture and use the QR code to add it to our
> wedding collection. It is completely optional—please let moments happen
> naturally and enjoy the party between photos.

## Pick the collection method before printing

A hunt fails when the card explains what to photograph but not where the
finished images should go. Text messages scatter submissions across different
conversations, and a public hashtag excludes guests who do not want to post.
A single [QR code for wedding photos](/en/qr-code-for-wedding-photos) gives
every table the same route.

Check the workflow on both an iPhone and an Android phone before sending the
cards to print. Test the QR code at the size it will appear, using ordinary
venue lighting and mobile data. Keep a short written link underneath as a
fallback for a damaged card or reluctant camera app.

If you want the scavenger hunt to feel like an analogue camera game, a
[digital disposable camera](/en/digital-disposable-camera-for-weddings) adds a
limited roll and removes the habit of checking every frame. OurFilm can reveal
the gallery immediately or when the event ends. It does not attach prompts or
scores to individual photos, so keep the game lightweight and judge favourites
afterward if you want a winner.

## Keep the prompts comfortable

Avoid tasks built around kissing, drinking, body shape, relationship status or
embarrassing a named guest. Do not ask people to photograph children unless a
parent or guardian has agreed. A prompt can be playful without making another
person the joke.

Tell guests if photographs may later be displayed or posted publicly. Sending
a picture to a private wedding gallery is not the same as consenting to social
media. Give the host a chance to review the collection before a slideshow or
public share.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many prompts should a wedding photo scavenger hunt have?

Put 10–15 prompts on one card, or five to six unique prompts on each table's
card. A longer list can stay in your planning document as a prompt bank. The
goal is to help guests notice moments, not make them finish homework.

### When should the photo scavenger hunt start?

Start during cocktail hour or at the reception, especially if the ceremony is
phone-free. Announce it once when the QR code and prompt cards are already in
front of guests. Avoid launching it during dinner service, speeches or a formal
dance.

### Do we need prizes or scoring?

No. A favourite-photo award or random draw is easier than checking every task
and encourages natural pictures instead of rushed proof. If you use bingo,
make one completed row enough to enter.

### How should guests submit their photos?

Use one QR code and state what happens after the scan. Some services accept
existing files, while digital guest cameras such as OurFilm are designed for
new photographs taken inside the event camera. Test the exact journey before
you print the cards.

### Can the scavenger hunt replace a wedding photographer?

No. A professional covers the essential moments, portraits, lighting and
backup process. The hunt adds candid viewpoints from around the room that the
couple and photographer cannot occupy at the same time.
