Family reunion photo sharing works best when relatives get one clear photo
destination before the first group picture. Use an upload album if you want
people to contribute old scans and pictures already saved on their phones. Use
a browser guest camera if the aim is to take a finite set of new pictures
together during the reunion.

The technology is only half the plan. A reunion can span several generations,
multiple households and relatives who rarely meet. Decide who owns the album,
who may see it, how children will be photographed and who will help anyone who
does not feel comfortable scanning a QR code.

## Choose between an archive and a shared camera

The phrase “family reunion album” can describe two different jobs. Combining
them without explanation is how guests scan a sign expecting to upload twenty
old pictures and instead find a camera—or open an upload page when they were
asked to take new photos.

### Use an upload album for old and existing pictures

Choose a conventional upload album when relatives should select files already
on their devices. It is the practical option for scanned prints, photographs
from earlier reunions, edited portraits and video. Check whether contributors
need an account, which file formats are accepted, whether captions survive and
who can remove an accidental upload.

Start this collection before the reunion. A relative with a box of labelled
prints needs time to scan or photograph them, and somebody should record names,
years and places before that context is lost. Do not make an elderly guest solve
that job beside the lunch table.

### Use a guest camera for new reunion moments

A digital guest camera opens a camera after the scan. Every relative receives a
limited roll and takes new pictures directly into the shared event. The limit
encourages people to notice different branches of the family rather than add a
whole camera roll of duplicates.

This approach suits the welcome, the big family portrait, the meal, games and
the farewells. It does not replace a historical archive or an unrestricted
upload folder. If both matter, give them separate names and instructions: one
place for the family history, one camera for today.

## Give the album an owner and a helper

Assign one organiser to create the event, test the settings, hold the full
download and respond to removal requests. Then name a second person as the
on-site helper. The helper can show relatives how to scan, move the QR card into
better light and notice when one table has not joined.

Do not make the oldest relative the default support desk simply because the
reunion celebrates them. Their job is to be present in the pictures. A younger
cousin or one person from each family branch can help without turning the day
into a technology lesson.

Agree on these points before sending the invitation:

- whether the gallery is for attendees only;
- whether relatives who could not travel may receive the finished link;
- who may download the complete collection;
- whether any picture may be posted publicly;
- who can approve photographs of children;
- how someone asks for a picture to be hidden or removed.

A QR code is a visual link, not a password. Keep it in the invitation, family
chat and private venue rather than on a public event page.

## Build the photo plan around the reunion timeline

### Arrival: capture households before people scatter

Put the first QR code after check-in or name tags, where guests have free hands.
Use the brighter first part of the gathering for one photograph of each
household or family branch. Ask one person to keep a simple list so nobody is
accidentally missed.

If a formal all-family portrait matters, assign a photographer and a time.
Guest pictures add candid viewpoints, but they are not a guarantee that every
face will be visible in one carefully lit frame.

### Family tree and old photographs: record the story

A family-tree display or table of old prints naturally creates conversations.
Ask guests to photograph the people telling the story, not only the object on
the table. A useful picture may show a grandparent identifying a place, cousins
comparing two generations or a child meeting a relative for the first time.

Do not promise that a guest camera will ingest an unlimited archive of saved
scans. Keep historical files in the upload album chosen for that purpose. The
reunion camera can document how the family reacts to them today.

### Meal: collect tables, details and the people serving

Before everyone begins eating, capture every table and the relatives who cooked,
served or organised the meal. Include place cards, handwritten recipes and the
dish that appears at every reunion. These details often explain the event more
clearly than another posed lineup.

Keep QR cards upright and away from sauces, drinks and serving dishes. A code
lying flat under a glass may exist without ever being scanned.

### Activities: assign viewpoints instead of running a contest

For a picnic, garden game, talent show or family quiz, give volunteers different
positions: start, finish, audience and behind the scenes. That produces a story
instead of several nearly identical pictures from the same side.

Use optional prompts that work across ages:

- three generations in one frame;
- the relative who travelled furthest;
- two cousins recreating an old picture;
- the hands preparing a family recipe;
- the person usually behind the camera;
- a quiet conversation away from the main table;
- the youngest and oldest willing participants together;
- the organisers after the checklist is finished;
- one detail future relatives should remember;
- the last group to leave.

Never turn age, mobility, family status or an old disagreement into a photo
challenge. Participation should remain optional, and an object or place can be
just as valuable as a portrait.

### Farewell: check the camera before everyone leaves

Make one short reminder while guests are still together. Confirm that queued
pictures have finished sending, especially at a rural venue with weak coverage.
Tell relatives exactly when the gallery will open and who will share it.

After the reveal, handle removal requests first. Then download the full
collection and keep a second copy outside the event service. A private gallery
is a collection point, not the only long-term family archive.

## Put the QR code where each generation pauses

Use the same final code in several practical locations:

- after the welcome or name-tag table;
- beside the family tree or old-photo display;
- on upright table cards;
- near the food station, away from spills;
- at the edge of the group-photo area;
- on a static slide before speeches;
- in the private invitation and family chat.

Print dark modules on a light, matte background with clear space around the
square. Add a short typed link underneath for anyone reading the invitation on
the same phone they will use to join. Test the printed version on iPhone and
Android using both venue Wi-Fi and mobile data.

Have the helper open the link for anyone who prefers assistance. Do not require
every relative to hold a smartphone: two people can share the same device only
if the selected service and its identity rules make that clear. With a guest
camera, it is usually simpler for the helper to take the requested picture on
their own roll and credit the idea verbally.

## Use instructions that describe the real action

For a saved-photo upload album:

> Add your family reunion favourites. Scan the code, choose pictures already on
> your phone and upload them to our private family album. Please add names and a
> year to older photographs where you can.

For a shared guest camera:

> Photograph today’s reunion through your eyes. Scan, enter your name and use
> your roll during the gathering. No app or account. Our private gallery opens
> when the reunion ends.

For the announcement:

> The same QR code is on the tables and beside the family tree. Joining is
> optional. Please ask before taking a close portrait, especially of children,
> and keep the finished gallery inside the family.

Do not use “upload your photos” for a camera that creates new pictures, and do
not promise video, filters, a live slideshow or unlimited saved-photo imports
unless the service you tested actually provides them.

## Protect children, older relatives and family history

Ask a parent or guardian before photographing a child and again before sharing
that image outside the private gallery. A group photograph at a family event is
not automatic permission for a public social post. Make declining easy and
avoid asking for an explanation.

Watch the background for addresses, school badges, medical information, travel
documents and captions on old photographs. A handwritten note can reveal a
full name, maiden name, date of birth or home address even when the people in
the picture are comfortable being photographed.

Explain the gallery to older relatives in plain language: who receives the
link, whether other guests see their pictures and how long the organiser plans
to keep the download. The [photo album privacy guide](/en/blog/wedding-photo-album-privacy)
provides a deeper consent and access checklist that also applies to a reunion.

## Family reunion photo-sharing checklist

- [ ] Old saved pictures and new event pictures have separate instructions.
- [ ] One organiser owns the gallery, download and backup.
- [ ] One helper can assist guests during the reunion.
- [ ] The audience, public-posting rule and removal route are clear.
- [ ] Parents or guardians know how children’s photos will be handled.
- [ ] The final printed QR code works on the venue connection.
- [ ] A formal all-family portrait has a named photographer and time.
- [ ] Guests know whether the gallery opens instantly or after the event.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the easiest way to share family reunion photos?

Give relatives one tested link or QR code before the event and one precise
instruction. Use an upload album for existing pictures and old scans, or a
browser guest camera for new photographs taken during the reunion. Assign an
organiser to download and back up the result.

### How can older relatives join a QR photo album?

Place the code in good light, print a short fallback link and name an on-site
helper. Let people watch one complete demonstration without taking their phone
away from them. Participation should be optional; a helper can take a requested
picture when someone does not want to join digitally.

### Where should I display the reunion photo QR code?

Use the welcome table, family-tree display, dining tables and group-photo area.
Keep cards upright, dry and well lit. Send the same direct link in the private
family chat because a phone cannot conveniently scan a code shown on its own
screen.

### Should the family reunion gallery open during or after the event?

An end-of-event reveal helps relatives stay in the conversation and turns the
album into a shared surprise. An instant gallery suits a group that deliberately
wants to browse during the day. State the choice beside the QR code.

### Can OurFilm collect old family photographs?

Not as an unlimited archive. OurFilm is primarily a camera for new event
pictures. A host may enable a 24-hour after-event saved-photo window, but every
addition uses a frame left on that relative’s original roll and video is not
accepted. Use a dedicated upload archive for a large historical collection.
