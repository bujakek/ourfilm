**Short answer:** Start with one disposable camera per reception table if you
want every table to have easy access. For a 100-person wedding seated at ten
tables, that means ten cameras, plus any you deliberately assign elsewhere.
If you only want a small collection of film memories, give fewer cameras to
named volunteers instead. There is no universal camera-to-guest ratio: your
seating plan, photo goal and full developing budget should decide the order.

## Count the places you want cameras, not just the guests

A camera is shared equipment, not a wedding favor for every person. Begin with
the final seating plan and choose one of two approaches.

**Table cameras:** Put a camera where each table can reach it, explain how to
pass it around and nominate someone to return it. This prioritizes access.
It does not guarantee that everyone will take a picture.

**Assigned cameras:** Ask a few willing guests to carry a camera for a specific
part of the day. One might cover getting ready, another the drinks reception
and another the evening. This prioritizes a manageable number of rolls, but
gives fewer people their own turn behind the camera.

[The Knot's disposable-camera guide](https://www.theknot.com/content/should-we-have-disposable-cameras)
includes both one camera per table of eight to ten and one per ten to fifteen
guests. Treat these as planning suggestions, not a measured participation
rate. A long banquet table and a room full of small tables need different
distribution plans even with the same guest count.

## Worked examples for 50, 100 and 150 guests

These are illustrative layouts, not recommended minimum purchases. Each
assumes ten people per table and one camera per table. The additional cameras
have named jobs; they are not a percentage added automatically to every order.

| Example seating plan    | Table cameras | Example total with assigned extras         |
| ----------------------- | ------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| 50 guests at 5 tables   | 5             | 6, including one for getting ready         |
| 100 guests at 10 tables | 10            | 12, including two for the drinks reception |
| 150 guests at 15 tables | 15            | 17, including two for the evening          |

For 100 guests at tables of eight, you would need thirteen table cameras,
not ten, if every table gets one. Start from your actual table count rather
than rounding a guest ratio down and leaving a table without a camera.

If that order is too expensive, change the distribution plan. For example,
four willing guests could each look after one camera and pass it among their
friends. Four cameras are then a deliberate film add-on, not a promise to
record every guest or every part of the wedding.

## Calculate frames separately from finished photographs

Read the exposure count on the exact camera you are buying. For a model with
27 exposures, ten cameras provide 270 shutter presses. That is capacity, not
270 photos you will necessarily keep: some frames may go unused, repeat the
same group or be too dark or blurred for your album.

Use these two calculations:

- **Available frames = cameras × exposures per camera.**
- **Planned camera order = table cameras + assigned extras + deliberate spares.**

Do not divide your ideal number of finished photos by the exposure count and
assume the result is a guaranteed camera requirement. There is no honest
universal success rate to insert into that calculation.

Instead, decide what a good result would look like: perhaps a handful of
photos from each friendship group, some room details and a few candid moments.
If you need reliable coverage of vows, speeches or particular family groups,
discuss that with your photographer rather than assigning it to unattended
disposable cameras.

## Budget for every roll you might send to the lab

Compare the total project cost before choosing a multipack:

**Cameras + development + scans + optional prints + delivery or travel.**

Ask the lab what is included in its quote. Development, digital scans and
paper prints are different outputs, and a low headline price may cover only
one stage. Confirm whether the quote is per camera or roll, whether partly
used rolls cost the same and how you receive the negatives and files.

As a made-up budgeting example, if your all-in quote were $30 per camera,
twelve cameras would cost $360 before any separately charged delivery. This
is arithmetic for comparing your own quotes, not a current supplier price
or a market average.

Set a maximum you are happy to spend on the film experiment. If adding one
camera to every table exceeds it, keep fewer physical cameras and choose a
different method for wider guest participation. Our
[film-versus-digital comparison](/en/blog/film-vs-digital-disposable-camera-wedding)
explains the difference in experience as well as logistics.

## Give each camera a handoff and a return plan

The buying decision is not finished until you know who brings the cameras home.

1. Number the cameras without covering the lens, flash, viewfinder or controls.
2. Keep a short list of which table or volunteer receives each one.
3. Add instructions for that model, including the flash and subject-distance
   guidance supplied with it. Demonstrate it to the people helping you.
4. Label a return container and name the person who collects it before venue
   cleanup begins. Make clear that the cameras are not take-home favors.
5. Check off the returned camera numbers and book the lab handoff.

Keep prompts light. “Photograph someone making your table laugh” gives a guest
something to notice; a long checklist can turn dinner into an assignment.
Use the [guest photo ideas](/en/blog/wedding-guest-photo-ideas) for a few optional
prompts, not a quota everyone has to complete.

## When a digital guest camera changes the calculation

If your real goal is a limited-roll experience for each guest rather than
physical film, you do not need to buy a camera per table. OurFilm opens a
shared event camera through a QR code or link. Guests enter their name and
take new photos in their phone browser, without installing an app or creating
an account.

The host chooses a finite roll length and whether the gallery is revealed
immediately or at the end of the capture window. Photos taken through the
camera join the event album. There is no import of photos already stored on
a guest's phone, and this is digital photography, not developed analogue film.

Your planning job becomes testing phones and venue connectivity, placing the
QR code and explaining the capture deadline. For example, a host-selected
24-shot roll gives a participating guest 24 opportunities; it does not mean
every invited person will join or use every shot.

You can also combine the methods: a few film cameras for people who love the
physical process, plus a digital guest camera for others. Keep the film-camera
return instructions separate from the QR instructions so guests know which
action belongs to which format.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many disposable cameras do I need for 100 wedding guests?

If you seat ten guests at each of ten tables and want one camera per table,
start with ten. Add cameras only for separate jobs or a deliberate spare plan.
Use fewer if film is a small add-on managed by named volunteers.

### Is one disposable camera per table enough?

It gives that table a shared camera, but it does not guarantee a turn or a
usable photo for everyone. Check the number of exposures and decide whether
you want casual table memories or broader coverage.

### Do I need a spare camera for every table?

Not automatically. Decide what would happen if a camera were lost or stopped
working, then choose a spare budget you can accept. More unused cameras do not
solve unclear instructions or missing collection arrangements.

### Are 270 exposures the same as 270 wedding photos?

No. Ten 27-exposure cameras provide 270 possible frames, but not all may be
taken or turn out as expected. Treat the figure as capacity, not a forecast
of finished photographs.

### Can a digital disposable camera replace physical cameras?

It can replace the per-table hardware when guests are happy to use phones.
It cannot replace the physical object, negatives or genuine film look. Choose
the format for the experience you actually want.
